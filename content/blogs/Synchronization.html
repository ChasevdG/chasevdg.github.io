Synchronization

The famous phrase from Donald Hebb's 1949 book The Organization of Behavior is "neurons that fire together, wire together." Hebbian learning became the framework where, if a pair of neurons repeatedly spike together, their synaptic connection strengthens and they become more likely to spike together in the future. This can be phrased as a synchronization problem where neurons will synchronize their activities for real data.

The goal of this blog post is as a pedagogical introduction to both Kuramoto models and Forward-Forward. However, as a destination, I will introduce Synchronizing Forward-Forward (the name still needs workshopping, canvasing, and focus groups) local learning model I did over a weekend. Using Kuramoto dynamics drastically reduces the number of nodes needed to get comparable performance to Hinton's model on MNIST and CIFAR100. That being said -- holding constistent with Hinton and forward-forward history -- it is not fully explored and ... gets slightly better results with the same number of nodes.

In the first part of this post, we will parouse through the theory of Kuramoto model-- the popular mathematical framework for understanding synchronization phenomena in interacting systems-- and it's generalizations. In the second part, I will go over Hinton's Forward-Forward algorithm and combine it with Kuramoto dynamics. In a later post, I will use the concepts introduced here to create a more general theory of local learning and connect information theoretic local learning to ECHO learning... but it may be a while before I have the freetime.

Why do we care?

Synchronization is an elegant way to express some of the intuitive phenomena we observe in the brain. If two neurons have similar spike trains, we assume that they are being used in the same computation. If one fires before the other, then the former is likely causing the later to fire.

From a neuroscience perspective, Kuramoto models specifically create an oscillatory behavior and -- when the network topology is restricted -- creates traveling waves akin to brainwaves. Moreover, the phase-locking behavior produces consistent offsets creating a phase precession phenomenon. By incorporating synchronization phenomena into artificial neural networks, we can make the network more "brain-like".

Similarly, the brain almost certainly does not learn through backprop; there is little evidence for backprop and plenty of evidence against. While backprop has worked well so far, it truly runs into problems when working with temporal data when one uses backprop through time (BPTT). For BPTT, the neurons have to store their activations for later use, and as the length scale increases, more and more activations need to be stored. It is much more biologically plausible for update steps to be localized.

But why do we even want a model to be more brain-like? Well, for a variety of reasons. For one, if the model is more brain-like we can start to reason about what the biological mechanisms of computation modes and what the effect of their failure are. For example, if synchronization and traveling waves are important for computation, then the biological mechanism acting as a conductor -- e.g. the medial septum -- would be crucial for maintaining synchronization. If the conductor begins to ware down, then the synchronization/coherence will be disrupted resulting in a degredation of that process. In the context of the medial septum and hippocampus, this effect is a degredation of spatial and temporal memory; making disrupted synchronization a candidate for inducing Alzheimer's.

Another reason to make it more biological is that the brain does things for a reason. While one can argue that image recognition is solved, handling streams of data has only gather focus in the last 5 years. However, we can look to the brain for insight. Local learning might be an important way to resolve data streams.

We can also find links that we were not looking for. For example, by making networks oscillatory and incorporating synchronization mechanics, one can eliviate some of the oversmoothing problem in graph neural networks. The same oversmoothing theory is being applied to length generalization, suggesting if one could incorporate synchronization methods, one may lead to better length generalization.

What is Synchronization?

Colloquially, synchronization is when things begin to coordinate their behavior in time. Think of a parade of marching soldiers, a flock of birds, or a school of fish: each individual is moving in its own way, but the group as a whole exhibits coordinated behavior. In the context of dynamical systems, synchronization refers to the process by which two or more systems adjust their rhythms due to interaction, leading to a common frequency or phase relationship. Take for example the applause of the audience in the Budapest Opera House (Fig 1a), where initially the clapping is random and uncoordinated, but after a while, the audience begins to clap in unison, creating a synchronized rhythm. This phenomenon is observed in a wide range of natural and engineered systems, from the flashing of fireflies (Fig 1b) to the operation of power grids and --most relevant for us-- can be used to model the firing of neurons in the brain.

Oscillators

Synchronization was first observed in Huygens pendulum clocks in 1665. Christiaan Huygens believed that two pendula would intuitively swing independently of each other at their own natural frequencies. However, he observed that two pendulums on a shared beam would eventually fall into step with each other, which was described as a sort of clock synchronization.

The physics of these pendulums can be nicely modeled by the same physics as spring block systems. Physicists call the general class of these systems as oscillators. Because the pendulums oscillate back and forth, the system or state, must be described by its location and direction of motion -- is it traveling toward or away from equalibrium (the center). This state is nicely captured by a complex circle; every position has a position (real value) and momentum (complex value). The coordinate on the circle can be uniquely described by an angle $\theta$ which is known as the phase. The speed at which the oscillator travels around this complex circle is known as the frequency -- which is controlled by the forces acting on the oscillator which, in the uncoupled case, is determined by the spring constant. The spring constant is often denoted $k$, however we will reserve $k$ for the coupling constants.

As the pendulums move back and forth they carry momentum. This momentum will be transfered into the beam and cause it to sway with the movement of the pendulums. This swaying of the beam affects the motion of the pendulums, creating a coupling between them. From the box-spring perspective with two oscillators coupling introduces a spring between two boxes with a coupling constant -- which is what we will mean when we use $K$. The frequencies of the oscillators while uncoupled is known as their natural frequency, $\omega$. The coupling disturbs the oscillators from their natural frequencies and eventually leads to the pendulums synchronizing. Synchronizing implies that the oscillators begin to have the same frequency, however they often have different phases.

The idea of oscillators extends past just pendulums and box-springs. A wide variety of periodic problems can be well modeled with oscillators. Most obviously, this is what made them so useful for clocks, but even the lighting up of fireflies and the dispersion of sound can be approximated with the same models. Though the mathematical modeling of coupling and synchronization itself was not formalized mathematically until Yoshiki Kuramoto in 1975, who proposed a simple model of coupled oscillators to capture the essence of synchronization.

Kuramoto Models

The Kuramoto model is an ordinary differential equation whose dynamics are one of the simplest models of coupled oscillators and phase synchronization. Importantly, the Kuramoto dynamics model only the phase of the oscillators and not the amplitudes. The core idea is that each oscillator increases its frequency to try to match its phase the other oscillators. As an example, when you are clapping after a concert, you may look to your neighbor and try to clap at the same time as them.

The update rule -- i.e. derivative -- for the phase, denoted by $\theta$, is given by, $$ \dot{\theta}_i = \omega_i + K\sum_{j=1}^{N} \sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) $$ where $\dot{\theta}_i$ is the time derivative of the phase of the $i$-th oscillator, $K$ denotes the coupling constant. The first term captures the oscillatation of an oscillator when there is no coupling and the second captures the interaction of oscillators.

Note that this describes the rate of change of the phase, but one could also describe it through the rate of change of the state. $$ \dot{z}_i = i\,\omega_i\, z_i + \frac{K}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(z_j - \bar{z}_j\, z_i^{2}\right), \qquad z_i = e^{i\theta_i},\ \ |z_i| = 1 $$ where $z$ is the complex number representing the state, $z_i = e^{i\theta_i}$, and $\bar{z}$ is the complex conjugate of the state -- $z = z_{re} + z_{im}i \implies \bar{z} = z_{re} - z_{im}i$.

Rather than calculating the distance to every other oscillator, one can instead use the mean field. The mean field $Z$ is given by, $$ Z = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} e^{i\theta_j}, $$ which can be used to define the mean phase, $\psi$, and order parameter $r$: $$ Z = r e^{i\psi}.$$ The order parameter $r \in [0,1]$ measures coherence -- $r \approx 0$ for scattered phases, $r = 1$ for perfect sync -- and $\psi$ indicated the mean phase. With these, one can formulate phase updates as, $$\dot{\theta}_i = \omega_i + K r \sin(\psi - \theta_i),$$ or the state update rule, $$\dot{z}_i = i\,\omega_i\, z_i + \frac{K}{2}\left(Z - \bar{Z}\, z_i^{2}\right).$$ where $\bar{Z}$ is the complex conjugate of the mean field parameter. Note that this reparameterization can happen only if the coupling constant is the same across oscillators.

Synchronization and Phase Locking

As time progresses, the oscillators can begin to phase-lock -- which is what is meant by synchronization. In Kuramoto models, synchronization does not mean the oscillators have the same phase. Rather, they end up rotating at the same frequency; holding a fixed offset from one another, thus being called phase-locking. Once locked, every oscillator advances in step with the mean field; its phase relative to the group stops changing.

Formally, a locked oscillator satisfies $\dot\theta_i = \dot\psi$ -- it rotates at exactly the mean-field frequency. Substituting this into the phase update rule and writing $\phi_i = \theta_i - \psi$ for the relative phase gives the fixed-point condition, $$ \sin\phi_i = \frac{\dot\psi - \omega_i}{K r}. $$ A real solution $\phi_i$ exists only when $|\dot\psi - \omega_i| \le K r$: oscillators inside this band settle at a constant offset and stay phase-locked forever, while oscillators whose natural frequency strays further away can never catch up -- $\phi_i$ perpetually drifts, sliding in and out of phase with the group. This is exactly the two-oscillator threshold $K > |\omega_1 - \omega_2| / 2$ seen earlier, generalized to the full population: as $K$ or $r$ grows, the locking band widens and pulls in oscillators that were drifting a moment before.

Increasing the coupling constant past this critical coupling induces a phase change where the system goes from random oscillations to coupling.

Minor Alterations

Changing Topology

So far we have assumed that all oscillators are fully connected with a shared coupling constant. One can instead make the oscillators interact only within their neighborhood. For example, one could connect box springs in a line where the boxes interact only with their neighborhood. We can reframe the phase update rule as, $$ \dot{\theta}_i = \omega_i + K\sum_{{\color{orange}j \in \Omega_i}} \sin(\theta_j - \theta_i), $$ where $\Omega_i$ is the neighborhood of the $i$-th node -- i.e. its connections. These neighborhoods can now be defined as a graph structure and we can abstract oscillators to nodes. Note that this can be scene as a complex-weighted graph with a particular form of convolution/message passing. Because nodes are only connected locally, one cannot use a mean field, but has to use the local average.

We can define a zoo of network topologies -- i.e. graph structures. For example, one can assume a 1D cyclic topology, a 2D grid/torus, a hyperbolic graph, etc. The rate of convergence and patterns created are affected by the network topology; for example, if the topology is a line, synchronization results in a traveling wave.

Weighted coupling

As the topology changes, one can allow two nodes having more than one spring between each other. This results in some nodes being more coupled then others; for example, nodes with two springs between them will have twice the coupling. This naturally generalizes to an abstraction with one spring between nodes, but with different coupling constants. $$ \dot{\theta}_i = \omega_i + \sum_{j=1}^{N} {\color{orange}k_{ij}} \sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) $$ In this formulation, one does not need to restrict to neighborhoods because the coupling constant is zero for unconnected nodes. Note, that coupling tends to be assumed positive by construction, so $k_{ij}$ is typically non-negative. One can extend this to negative numbers which would try to align two oscillators anti-phase.

Stochastic Form

Kuramoto models are a part of the field known as Interacting Particle Systems and statistical mechanics. To make it more "realistic" a noise parameter is sometimes included into the dynamics, $$ \dot{\theta}_i = {\color{orange}\mathcal{N} (\omega_i, \sigma)} + K \sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_j - \theta_i). $$ While I have used a normal distribution in this equation (white noise), this noise can be from any distribution and has been studied in fractional diffusion/rough noise settings.

The Lohe Generalization

While we have seen that we can generalize to different network topologies, we can also generalize from unit complex numbers to N-D unit vectors. That is, instead of oscillators traveling around a circle, we can let them travel around a sphere. This generalization is known as a swarm sphere or a Lohe sphere. The more general form generalizing to unitary matrices is known as the Lohe model.

Rather than using the $\sin$ between 2D vectors, we can generalize to $N$-dimensions by projecting vectors onto eachother. The projection gives the similarity between the two vectors, given by the cosine; the difference between the vectors will be the sine. This allows us to generalize the Kuramoto update rule as, $$ \dot{x}_i = \Omega_i x_i + \frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \Big(x_j - (x_i\cdot x_j)\,x_i\Big), \qquad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d,\ \|x_i\| = 1 $$ where $\Omega_i$ is a skew-symmetric generator -- i.e. $\Omega \in \mathfrak{so}(N)$ -- generalizing the (2D) $i\omega$ natural rotation to $N$-dimensional rotations -- i.e. N-dimensional special orthogonal matrices, $SO(N)$.

Within the sum is a projection: $x_j - (x_i\cdot x_j)\,x_i$ which computes the component of $x_j$ perpendicular to $x_i$, i.e. the tangential pull of $x_j$ on the sphere at $x_i$. This is exactly the generalization of $\sin(\theta_j-\theta_i)$, which is the perpendicular component of $z_j$ relative to $z_i$ on the unit circle.

Projection formulas of this kind are ubiquitous and in later posts I will connect this to Barlow theory of efficient coding and Oja's rule -- which marks the precursor to independent component analysis. This equation also has deep connections to equivariant/group commutator and if written in Clifford algebra is quite clean 👌 (though this will take some time to work towards)

This equation also can be connected to Transformers. Since we have an inner-product $x_i\cdot x_j$, we can apply the kernel trick and replace it with $k(q_j, k_i)$ making the coupling part of the equation strikingly familiar to attention. Though once again, I will elaborate more in a later post, but you can see ... for the connections.

Stuart-Landau Oscillators

Kuramoto and Lohe both fix the oscillator's amplitude by construction -- $z_i$ is confined to the unit circle (or $x_i$ to the unit hypersphere) and only the phase is allowed to move. Coupling, as a result, can only ever push phases around; it has nothing to say about how "strongly" an oscillator is oscillating. In terms of box-springs, we are describing how fast the box oscillates back and forth, but not how far it is allowed to deviate from equalibrium. The natural next generalization is to couple the oscillators in both phase and space (amplitude).

This generalization leads directly to Stuart-Landau oscillators. The dynamics of a single oscillator uncoupled oscillator evolves as $$ \dot{z} = (\mu + i\omega)z - |z|^2 z, \qquad z \in \mathbb{C}, $$ where $\mu$ is a growth/decay rate controlling the convergence behavior of the amplitude, and $\omega$ is the natural frequency controlling the rotation speed. If the cubic term, $|z|^2 z$ is greater than $\mu$, then the amplitude will shrink, but if it smaller than the amplitude will grow. When $\mu \le 0$ the only stable state is the origin, however when $\mu > 0$ the stable state forms a ring at the radius $\mu$ which all states converge to known as a (stable) ring attractor or limit cycle. The value $\mu=0$ where solutions (attractors) go from stable points complex rings is known as the Hopf bifurcation.

If an oscillator moves off of the limit cycle in the amplitude direction, then it will be pushed back on. However, it is free to move along the cycle itself. This free direction of motion, to my understanding, is what Max Welling refers to as the Goldstone Mode in his ICLR 2026 Keynote.

Coupling a population of these oscillators through the mean field, $$ \dot{z}_i = (\mu + i\omega_i)z_i - |z_i|^2 z_i + K\bigl(\langle z \rangle - z_i\bigr), \qquad \langle z \rangle = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N z_j, $$ now pulls oscillators toward each other's positions in the plane, not just their phases. Just as with Kuramoto, this mean-field is shorthand for an explicit all-to-all pairwise coupling, $$ \dot{z}_i = (\mu + i\omega_i)z_i - |z_i|^2 z_i + \frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \bigl(z_j - z_i\bigr), $$ which expands to the same equation once you pull the sum apart -- $\frac{1}{N}\sum_j (z_j - z_i) = \langle z\rangle - z_i$. Written this way it is clear the mean-field is just the special case where every oscillator is coupled to every other with the same weight $K/N$; restricting the sum to a neighborhood $\Omega_i$, as we did for Kuramoto, gives the same graph-coupled generalization here. This buys a genuinely new phenomenon that phase-only Kuramoto cannot produce: amplitude death. When the coupling $K$ is strong and the natural frequencies are spread widely enough, the coupling term can overpower each oscillator's own tendency to orbit, dragging the whole ensemble into the now-stable fixed point at the origin -- the population stops oscillating altogether rather than merely failing to phase-lock.

Synchronization models are widely recognized to be revived by Max Welling's group in Löwe et al. ... 's work on complex autoencoders. Generative Modeling

Recent work by ..., used synchronization as an alternative to diffusion models. The idea is that, by reversing the direction of the Kuramoto dynamics, one can force desynchronization pushing the distribution toward a von Mises (wrapped Gaussian) distribution. This desynchronization (essentially diffusing over the circle) is analogous to adding noise in diffusion models.

Hinton's Forward-Forward

Local Learning

A different kind of local, self-organizing dynamics: Hinton's Forward-Forward algorithm replaces backprop's forward + backward pass with two forward passes. An MNIST digit is fed through the network twice — once with the correct label written into its first pixels (positive data), once with a wrong label (negative data). Each layer optimizes only its own objective $p = \sigma\bigl(\sum_j y_j^2 - \theta\bigr)$, raising its goodness (the sum of squared activations) above the threshold $\theta$ on positive data and pushing it below on negative data. No gradients travel between layers; each learns locally.

Spherical Swarm Models

The Lohe demo earlier (Fig 5) is only one of three ways a swarm can self-organize on the sphere $S^2$. Below, each model runs as its own diagram so they can be compared directly — they differ in what the agents come to agree on: a shared orientation, a location coloured by phase, or a common heading.

Lohe oscillators

Each agent is a unit vector that spins about the vertical axis at its own natural frequency $\omega_i$, while coupling $K$ pulls every vector toward the mean field. With uniform frequencies the whole swarm co-rotates and locks into a single precessing cluster at any $K$; with normal-distributed frequencies each vector precesses about its own axis, so the coupling must fight the spread — the Kuramoto competition — and only large $K$ collapses them into one cluster. The order parameter $r=\lvert\frac1N\sum_i \mathbf{x}_i\rvert$ runs from 0 (scattered) to 1 (one cluster).

Swarmalators

Swarmalators add an internal phase $\theta_i$ (shown as colour) that co-evolves with position. Space coupling makes agents aggregate; the term $J\cos(\theta_j-\theta_i)$ makes that attraction depend on phase similarity, while phase coupling $K$ syncs the colours of nearby agents. Positive $J$ gives a synced ball of one colour; negative $J$ splits space by phase into coloured patches — behaviour Lohe sync cannot produce.

Flocking (Vicsek)

Vicsek agents instead carry a velocity and move along geodesics, steering each step toward the average heading of their neighbours (plus noise). They agree on a direction of motion, not a location, so order shows up as migrating flocks and rotating polar vortices. The order parameter here is the polarization $\lvert\frac1N\sum_i \mathbf{v}_i\rvert$.
